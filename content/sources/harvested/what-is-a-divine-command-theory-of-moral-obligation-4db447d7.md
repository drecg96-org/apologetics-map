# Agent summary

Evans begins from Robert Adams's social theory of obligation, according to which obligations in general are constituted by social relationships. He then argues that moral obligations are grounded in the distinctive social relation humans bear to God and can therefore be understood as divine commands or requirements.

A useful refinement is Evans's distinction between a modal-status thesis and a divine-discretion thesis. He argues that the former is essential to divine-command theory while the latter is not, allowing a view on which God's commands remain essential to obligation even when what God commands is necessary rather than arbitrary. He also argues that divine requirements can be communicated through means such as conscience rather than only special revelation.

## How to use this source

This is a direct philosophical defense of the relational/divine-command account of obligation. It supports the claim that duties have an interpersonal-authoritative structure, but that premise and its theistic interpretation remain contested.
