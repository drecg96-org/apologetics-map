# Agent summary

Lee and Evans describe divine-command theories as accounts on which moral obligations are caused by, constituted by, or identical with divine commands. A major strand draws on Robert Adams's social theory of obligation: obligations generally arise within relations between persons, and moral obligation is then explained through a distinctive relation between human persons and God.

The chapter is especially useful for distinguishing divine-command theory from a crude theological voluntarism in which whatever God happens to will becomes good. It surveys desiderata for an adequate theory of obligation, explains why proponents think a divine-command account can satisfy them, and reviews major objections and possible responses.

## How to use this source

Use it primarily for the relational character of moral obligation and for contemporary divine-command theory as a grounding proposal. It is a scholarly survey of a contested theory, not evidence by itself that divine-command theory or theism is true.
