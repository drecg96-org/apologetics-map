# Argument

The strongest version of a resurrection argument should not begin with:

> The Bible says Jesus rose, therefore Jesus rose.

Nor should it label every disputed Gospel detail an "established fact."

Instead it asks whether a comparatively modest historical core is best explained by a real resurrection.

## Stage 1 — identify the historical problem

The first inference combines:

1. Jesus' death by crucifixion;
2. experiences or reports that early followers interpreted as encounters with the risen Jesus;
3. the very early emergence of resurrection proclamation.

That yields an intermediate conclusion: these claims form a connected historical pattern that calls for explanation.

## Stage 2 — compare explanations

The second inference is abductive.

A resurrection would unify the pattern if it occurred. Natural alternatives can explain portions of the data, but the Christian case argues that the leading alternatives incur significant explanatory costs or require a multi-part story.

The question is therefore not merely whether resurrection **can** explain the data. It is whether it explains them **better overall** once background plausibility, evidential quality, rival explanations, and historical method are all considered.

## Main pressure points

The argument can fail at several distinct places:

- one or more historical premises may be weaker than claimed;
- appearance experiences may be explainable without an external risen Jesus;
- early resurrection proclamation may arise from Jewish religious categories plus visionary experience;
- multiple natural causes may be perfectly acceptable historically;
- a miracle may have a very low prior probability;
- or historical method may be unable to identify divine agency even if the event occurred.

The formal mapper keeps those disputes separate so a critic can say exactly what they grant and where they stop.
