# Argument

A strong Van Tilian version of the transcendental argument is not intended to conclude merely that "some god exists." It aims at a more ambitious conclusion: **the Christian worldview is the necessary precondition for intelligible thought and experience.**

A simplified reconstruction is:

1. Rational discourse and knowledge actually occur.
2. Rational discourse presupposes conditions such as objective logical norms, meaningful predication, an intelligible order, and a dependable relation between human minds and the world.
3. A worldview must be able to account for those conditions without undermining them.
4. The Christian worldview claims that a rational, sovereign Creator made and sustains an ordered world, made human beings in his image, and reveals himself to creatures.
5. Rival autonomous worldviews are alleged to be unable to supply an internally adequate foundation for those same conditions.
6. Therefore, Christian theism is argued to be the necessary precondition of intelligibility.

## Where the argument actually carries weight

The crucial premise is not simply that Christianity contains a Creator. The difficult step is **5**: showing that relevant rival worldviews cannot account for the conditions of rationality while Christianity can.

That is why this argument cannot responsibly be presented as:

> We use logic; therefore Christianity.

It requires a comparative worldview argument.

## Transcendental rather than probabilistic

Van Tilian presentations often call the argument "indirect" or "transcendental." The intended claim is that denying the Christian foundation while continuing to reason depends on intellectual conditions that, on this account, the denial itself cannot justify.

That is stronger than an inference to the best explanation, and therefore it carries a heavier burden. A critic can challenge whether the proposed conditions are genuinely necessary, whether non-Christian accounts really fail, or whether the conclusion reaches reality rather than only necessary beliefs or conceptual commitments.

## Christian grounding versus public premise

Romans 1, Genesis 1, and Colossians 2 explain why a Christian might expect rational creatures in an intelligible creation. In public argument, however, those passages should not be treated as premises the non-Christian must accept before the argument begins. The philosophical work is to show why the worldview described by those texts succeeds or fails as an account of intelligibility.
