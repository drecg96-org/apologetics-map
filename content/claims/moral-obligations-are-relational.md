# Claim

Obligations seem different from mere descriptions of what is valuable. To have an obligation is to be under a requirement, and many theistic ethicists argue that requirements are naturally understood in social or relational terms.

C. Stephen Evans develops a social account in which moral obligations are grounded in the relation between humans and God.

## Pressure point

This is not universally accepted. Secular realists can argue that normative reasons or obligations are basic facts and do not need a commander, while naturalists can attempt to ground normativity in facts about rational agency or flourishing.

A useful test question is: **why think every binding reason must be a demand made by a person?** We can have reasons generated by facts, relationships, promises, or rational consistency without first describing each reason as a command.

The theistic reply is that specifically *moral obligation* has a second-personal or authoritative structure that impersonal facts struggle to capture. The secular reply is that this structure is asserted rather than demonstrated.

So the relational character of obligation is a premise to defend, not something the moral argument may simply assume.
