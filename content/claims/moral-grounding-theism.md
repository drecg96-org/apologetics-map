# Claim

If objective values and duties exist, a personal and necessarily good foundation may explain them better than an ultimately impersonal reality.

The strongest form of the claim is comparative rather than dismissive: theism is proposed as a better explanation of moral value and obligation than its competitors.

## Why a personal ground is proposed

Theists often distinguish values from duties. Values are tied to the character of the good; duties have an authoritative or relational character, as things persons owe or are required to do.

On this approach, God's necessary nature grounds value, while divine commands or requirements can ground obligation.

This gives the theistic account two distinct explanatory jobs:

1. **Value:** why persons, love, justice, courage, and other goods possess objective worth.
2. **Obligation:** why some reasons have authoritative force as things persons genuinely ought to do.

The distinction matters because an impersonal realm of moral facts may explain value without obviously explaining the relational or authoritative character of duty, while critics deny that duty needs a commander at all.

## Main challenges

The strongest next moves are not "atheists can be moral." They are:

- **Secular realism:** necessary normative facts may exist without God.
- **Naturalism:** moral properties may reduce to or be grounded in natural facts.
- **Euthyphro:** does divine grounding make goodness arbitrary or independent of God?
- **Stopping-point objection:** does saying "God's nature is the Good" explain goodness or simply rename the ultimate brute fact?
- **Epistemology:** even if God grounds morality, how do humans reliably know the relevant moral truths or divine requirements?

## Limit

Even if successful, this conclusion does not establish Christianity, the Incarnation, the Resurrection, or the authority of a particular scripture. It would support something closer to a necessarily good personal moral foundation.
