# Question

Ordinary reasoning assumes a great deal before any particular argument begins. We rely on logical norms, meaningful predication, memory, a sufficiently stable relation between mind and world, and standards that distinguish good inference from bad inference.

The transcendental question is not merely **"What caused these things?"** It is:

**What must be true for rational thought, knowledge, and argument to be possible at all?**

## Why this is a different kind of argument

A transcendental argument begins with something the interlocutor is expected to grant—such as experience, reasoning, language, or knowledge—and asks what conditions are necessary for that activity to be possible.

That structure matters because the argument is not simply adding God as one more explanatory hypothesis alongside other hypotheses. Its stronger forms claim that some disputed reality is a necessary precondition of the very reasoning used to dispute it.

## Questions to separate

This broad issue should fan out rather than be treated as one slogan:

- Are logical laws objective, necessary, and normatively binding?
- Can human knowledge be trustworthy if minds and the world have no deeper rational relation?
- What, if anything, justifies induction and expectations of regularity?
- Are epistemic "oughts"—such as believing according to evidence—objective?
- Does a theistic worldview explain these conditions better than naturalistic or non-theistic alternatives?
- Even if theism helps, does the argument identify Christianity specifically?

The last question is deliberately separate. Moving from intelligibility to **God** and moving from **God** to **Christianity** are not automatically the same inference.
