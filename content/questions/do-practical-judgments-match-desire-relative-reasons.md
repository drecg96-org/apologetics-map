# Diagnostic question

Suppose you have said that a completely indifferent person may have **no categorical moral reason** not to harm an innocent person.

Now consider the way you actually make moral judgments. When you say of a cruel person,

> They should not do that even if they want to, approve of it, and can get away with it,

what do you mean?

Is that merely shorthand for your own disapproval, your society's rules, or a strategy for producing behavior you prefer? Or do you mean that there is still a reason **for that person** not to do it even when the person's desires point the other way?

## If you mean the agent still has a reason

That creates a real tension with the earlier answer.

It does not prove moral realism by itself, and it need not show hypocrisy. But the theoretical claim was that the indifferent agent lacks a desire-independent reason, while the practical judgment now treats such a reason as present.

The next question is whether the practical judgment should be revised or whether the theory of reasons was too restrictive.

## If you mean only standpoint-relative condemnation

The position can remain consistent.

You may still condemn, punish, restrain, persuade, or organize society against the action. What you are declining to say is that a stance-independent reason applies to the agent regardless of the agent's motivational standpoint.

That is a substantive consequence rather than a contradiction.

## If you keep using categorical moral language without believing it literally

That can also be a coherent strategy. Error theorists and other anti-realists can distinguish the literal truth of moral claims from the practical usefulness of continuing moral language and practices.

So "you still live morally" is not, by itself, a refutation of anti-realism.

## Useful follow-up questions

- If the person's desires changed completely, would "they still should not do it" remain true?
- Are you describing a reason **you have to stop them**, or a reason **they have not to act**?
- If every relevant individual and society approved of the act, would anyone still be morally mistaken?
- When you call someone morally wrong, do you mean more than "their values conflict with mine or ours"?

These questions test whether the person's practical judgments actually fit the theory they have accepted without assuming in advance that ordinary moral practice proves realism.
