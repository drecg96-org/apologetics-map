# Objection

The self-exclusion response can fail if it treats these as equivalent:

1. **I believe God exists and I reject relationship with him.**
2. **I do not believe God exists, but I would welcome relationship with God if I became convinced.**

Those are not obviously the same act of will.

Philosophers of religion often call the second category **nonresistant nonbelief**: lack of belief without culpable resistance to relationship with God.

## Why this matters for hell

If a person does not know that God exists, they may be unable to choose either union with or separation from God in the fully informed sense the free-will defense seems to require.

So the Christian cannot simply say:

> "Everyone in hell chose to be there."

The next question is **what kind of choice, knowledge, resistance, or culpability is actually required**.

The problem becomes sharper if belief is not something a person can directly produce at will. Responsibility may instead have to attach to things such as inquiry, attention, intellectual honesty, avoidance, or resistance.

## Connection to divine hiddenness

This branch also connects the problem of hell to the problem of divine hiddenness.

If a perfectly loving God wants relationship with every willing person, why do apparently willing people sometimes remain unconvinced that God exists at all?
