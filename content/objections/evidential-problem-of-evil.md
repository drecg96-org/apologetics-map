# Objection

A strong contemporary problem of evil need not claim:

> "If any evil exists, God cannot exist."

The harder skeptical claim is comparative and evidential:

> The kinds, amount, and distribution of suffering we observe look less expected if an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God governs the world than they do on at least some non-theistic alternatives.

## Why a free-will defense may not finish the debate

A free-will defense can be relevant to moral evil caused by human choices. But the skeptic can press further:

- animal suffering long before human beings,
- disease, disasters, and other natural suffering,
- apparently excessive or horrendous suffering,
- and cases where no outweighing good is visible.

Showing that God *could* have a reason to permit suffering establishes a kind of logical compatibility. The evidential objection asks the additional question of whether the observed world is what we should reasonably expect given perfect goodness and power.

## What a response must do

A serious Christian response should therefore say more than "free will."

It needs to address the inference from **apparently gratuitous evil** to **evidence against theism**, including the limits of human perspective, proposed goods that might justify permission, and whether those replies actually change the comparative probability.
