# Response

A stricter Christian answer says the objection starts from the wrong criterion.

On this view, the decisive issue is not whether a person is comparatively kind, sincere, or morally admirable. Human beings are reconciled to God **through Christ**, and saving faith is not interchangeable with general moral decency.

The Westminster Confession gives a clear confessional example: diligent conformity to natural light or another religion is not treated as an independent path to salvation.

## What this answer explains

It explains why Christianity can consistently say both:

1. morally admirable behavior does not earn heaven; and
2. saving relation to Christ is normatively decisive.

The view therefore rejects the idea that God is running a simple moral scorecard and then arbitrarily subtracting points for unbelief.

## What still needs to be defended

The restrictivist claim does **not by itself settle the fairness objection**.

The next question remains:

> Why is the absence of saving faith justly attributable to the sincere nonbeliever?

A complete defense therefore needs some further account of revelation, grace, human responsibility, resistance, or divine election. Otherwise it states the condition for salvation without yet explaining why the condition is just in the hardest cases.
