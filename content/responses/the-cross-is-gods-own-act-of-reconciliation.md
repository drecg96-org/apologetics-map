# Response

The first clarification is that, in historic Trinitarian Christianity, the cross is not normally supposed to be a transaction in which **one helpless party pays off a stronger being who has God trapped by a rule**.

The Christian claim is that **God is acting in Christ to reconcile humanity to God**.

That changes the shape of the objection. The question becomes less:

> "Why did God need somebody else to pay him?"

and more:

> "Why did God choose self-giving incarnation, death, and resurrection as the way to overcome sin and restore communion?"

## Christianity does not have only one atonement theory

The New Testament uses several overlapping motifs, and Christian traditions weight them differently:

- sacrifice and expiation,
- justification and substitution,
- victory over sin, death, and hostile powers,
- reconciliation,
- healing and participation,
- moral transformation and the revelation of divine love.

So it is too strong to claim that Christianity has one universally agreed philosophical explanation showing that God **could not possibly** forgive by any other route.

A more modest claim is that the cross is the redemptive act Christianity says God actually chose, and that its meaning includes more than a bare administrative cancellation of a penalty.

## What remains open

This still leaves a powerful follow-up:

> If God has already acted graciously to reconcile the world, why not simply make every person saved by default?

That moves the debate from the **possibility of forgiveness** to the nature of **reconciliation, participation, freedom, and final salvation**.
