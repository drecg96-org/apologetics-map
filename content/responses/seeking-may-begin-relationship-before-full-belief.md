# Response

Schellenberg's argument relies partly on the thought that a person cannot begin a conscious meaningful relationship with God without first believing that God exists.

Some Christian philosophers challenge that assumption.

A person can pray conditionally, seek, hope, lament, participate in worship, respond to conscience, or address the possibility of God without yet possessing settled propositional belief.

Michael Rea develops a particularly strong version: someone may be able to **try to participate** in relationship with God even without possessing the standard Christian concept of God, and that trying may itself constitute a form of participation.

## Why this matters

If some relationship can begin before full belief, then nonbelief does not automatically entail that God has made relationship unavailable.

That weakens one step in the hiddenness argument without requiring the claim that every nonbeliever is resistant.

## Limit

This is not a complete solution.

A person may sincerely seek, pray, hope, and remain met—so far as they can tell—by silence. At some point, prolonged absence may itself damage the possibility of trust and relationship.

So the response shifts the debate from:

> "No belief means no relationship is possible."

to:

> "How much cognitive clarity does perfect love owe a sincere seeker, and when does hiddenness become relationally defeating?"
