# Response

A second response asks whether the hiddenness argument assumes the wrong goal for revelation.

If God's aim were simply:

> "Make sure every rational person assents to the proposition that God exists,"

then maximally clear public evidence would seem natural.

But Christian theism usually says God seeks something thicker: reconciliation, trust, repentance, love, holiness, and communion.

Moser therefore argues that we should expect divine evidence to be **purposive**—given in forms suited to the kind of relationship God seeks—rather than merely "spectator evidence" that allows a detached observer to settle God's existence while remaining otherwise unchanged.

## Why this could matter

On this account, some epistemic distance may prevent relationship with God from being reduced to passive observation of an obvious cosmic object.

Divine revelation might instead address the whole person: intellect, will, conscience, loves, and conduct.

## The major limit

This response becomes weak or morally suspect if it simply says:

> "Anyone unconvinced by God must be unwilling to change."

That would assume away nonresistant nonbelief.

A sincere seeker could be morally serious, willing to be transformed, and still honestly unconvinced. So purposive revelation can be part of a Christian explanation, but it still must account for why such a person would sometimes remain without enough evidence even to identify the one seeking relationship with them.
