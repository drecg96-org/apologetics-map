# Response

One possible reason for **some** divine hiddenness is morally significant freedom.

The careful version of this argument should not say:

> "If God were clearly real, nobody could freely reject him."

The map already has reasons to reject that claim. Knowledge of existence is not identical to love, trust, worship, or obedience.

Murray's stronger point is about **practical pressure**.

If a person were constantly and unmistakably aware of an omniscient, omnipotent moral judge—and equally aware that obedience leads to supreme reward while rebellion leads to catastrophic loss—then at least some choices might begin to resemble behavior under overwhelming surveillance or threat.

On that view, a degree of epistemic distance could create room for motives, loyalties, and character to develop without every action being dominated by immediate awareness of divine consequences.

## What this explains

At most, this could explain why God might avoid **maximally overwhelming disclosure**.

It can also make sense of a world in which evidence is sufficient for inquiry and trust but not so psychologically dominating that every ordinary decision is made under felt divine inspection.

## What it does not explain

It does **not** yet explain why a sincere seeker would lack enough evidence even to believe that God exists.

That is a much harder case.

The response must therefore face the next objection: if people can know that God exists and still reject, resent, disobey, or refuse him, why would protecting freedom require uncertainty about God's existence in the first place?
